## Synthesis for the Premier League project

The strongest common foundation is a transparent relative-value signal, causal estimation, cost-sensitive decisions, finite-horizon exits and chronological evaluation. Financial-price mean reversion cannot be assumed for bounded match contracts. A bookmaker-derived reference probability is not an executable hedge, and a shrinking discrepancy can result from the reference moving rather than the held contract gaining value. The strongest project therefore tests actual prediction-market price adjustment and books cash P&L from entry asks and exit bids. FPL-related information belongs in a small, separately evaluated extension.

The reviewed papers repeatedly distinguish forecasting from tradable profitability and warn about transaction costs, changing relationships, model search and optimistic backtests. A negative net result is informative. Complex copulas, wavelets, particle filters, deep learning and market-making models should not all be combined into one student project.

### 1. A Profit Model for Spread Trading with an Application to Energy Futures

**Authors/date:** Takashi Kanamura, Svetlozar T. Rachev, Frank J. Fabozzi. KIT Working Paper No. 27, May 2011; underlying manuscript dated October 19, 2009. **Length:** 31 PDF pages.

The authors express expected spread-trading profit as the sum of two components: profit when a spread first reaches its convergence target before a deadline, and the gain or loss from forced liquidation when convergence has not occurred. Transition densities and first-hitting-time densities make this framework applicable beyond a particular instrument. For energy calendar spreads, they estimate AR(1) dynamics, test for unit roots, map the estimates to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model, and use an approximate hitting-time expansion. An empirical illustration uses daily NYMEX WTI, heating-oil, and natural-gas futures, April 2000–March 2008, with 120-day formation/trading windows rolled by 20 days. Natural-gas spreads show the highest average profits and reported risk-adjusted performance; mean reversion, volatility, and seasonality help explain differences. These results are historical associations rather than a universal implication that higher volatility improves trading. Overlapping windows, simplified execution, constant OU parameters, and costs considered largely outside the model limit practical extrapolation. A later regime-switching exercise also finds mean reversion may weaken in the high-volatility regime.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Expected P&L must include nonconvergence and deadline liquidation (pp. 5–8); AR(1), unit-root tests, and OU mapping (pp. 9–12); formation/trading separation and two-standard-deviation entry (p. 14); regime-switching caveat (pp. 17–18). For sports, these support studying a *cross-market discrepancy* with an explicit remaining horizon, not assuming an individual match probability reverts to a fixed mean. Costs must be deducted from executable trades (p. 8). **Extraction:** All prose/tables readable; several plots are not reproduced legibly by extraction. Numeric parameter typos appear between prose and tables (e.g., p. 13 versus Table 7 on p. 27); use the table with explicit qualification, not false precision.

### 2. Arbitrage under Power

**Authors/date:** Michael Boguslavsky and Elena Boguslavskaya, February 2004 (short version in *Risk*, June 2004). **Length:** 15 PDF pages. The filename adds “mean reversion,” but the paper title is *Arbitrage under Power*.

This paper solves finite-horizon position management for an OU spread and power utility of terminal wealth. With zero rates, no transaction costs, unconstrained long/short trading, and known parameters, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation has an explicit solution. The optimal position is proportional to current wealth and opposite to the spread, multiplied by a factor depending on risk aversion and time remaining. A trader initially increases exposure as a spread moves away from equilibrium, but eventually reduces a losing position as shrinking wealth dominates the widening opportunity. Relative to logarithmic utility, more risk-averse investors reduce exposure as the horizon approaches, whereas less risk-averse investors can increase it. Monte Carlo experiments show asymmetric sensitivity to parameter errors: overestimating mean-reversion speed or underestimating noise encourages excessive risk. This is a theoretical benchmark, not an implementable betting rule. Perfect liquidity, continuous adjustment, unrestricted shorting, and a stationary unbounded spread are central assumptions; the authors explicitly acknowledge regime changes, jumps, estimation error, and market impact as omitted features.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Known-date convergence may suit a Brownian bridge better than stationary OU (p. 2); model/wealth assumptions (pp. 3–4); explicit control and wealth-dependent unwinding (pp. 5–6); horizon effects (pp. 9–10); conservative parameter estimation (pp. 10–12). For sports contracts, the useful lesson is horizon-aware exposure and uncertainty, not applying an OU model directly to a bounded probability approaching binary settlement. **Extraction:** Full text present. Some equations have symbol/layout corruption; no potentially corrupted displayed formula is copied as implementation-ready code here.

### 3. Identifying Small Mean Reverting Portfolios

**Author/date:** Alexandre d’Aspremont, February 26, 2008. **Length:** 24 PDF pages.

The paper seeks strongly mean-reverting portfolios while limiting their number of constituent assets. Dense cointegration or canonical-correlation portfolios can be expensive to trade, difficult to interpret, and so low in variation that their apparent opportunities lie inside bid–ask spreads. Predictability under a vector autoregression is represented as a generalized Rayleigh quotient; minimizing it proxies mean reversion. Adding a cardinality limit produces a difficult combinatorial problem. The paper develops a greedy asset-selection method and a semidefinite relaxation, then uses sparse inverse-covariance estimation and LASSO estimates of VAR coefficients to stabilize parameters and identify smaller dependency clusters. Examples with U.S. swaps and foreign exchange show favorable sparsity-versus-reversion trade-offs and improved out-of-sample behavior from penalization. Critically, promising frictionless swap Sharpe ratios disappear with even a one-basis-point spread. The appendix explains OU estimation, half-life, utility-based sizing, and leverage constraints. The work supports simple, interpretable baskets but does not establish that mathematically fast reversion implies an economically tradeable opportunity.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Costs and economically insignificant variation (pp. 1–2); sparse generalized eigenvalue methods (pp. 3–9); regularized estimation (pp. 9–13); explicit out-of-sample windows (pp. 16–17); disappearance of profitability at 1 bp (p. 17 and Figure 10, p. 24); OU half-life (p. 18). For sports, payoff-equivalent contracts offer a more interpretable starting point than optimizing arbitrary baskets of different matches. **Extraction:** All prose, tables, and appendix present; plot labels and mathematical spacing are imperfect but the findings are clear in text.

### 4. Mixed Copula Pairs Trading Strategy on the S&P 500

**Authors/date:** Fernando A. B. Sabino da Silva, Flavio A. Ziegelmann, João F. Caldeira. ResearchGate cover labels an April 2017 working paper, *A Pairs Trading Strategy Based on Mixed Copulas*, with upload February 22, 2019; the attached manuscript has the title above and cites 2018 research. Date/version is therefore not unambiguous. **Length:** 32 PDF pages, including the repository cover.

This study compares distance-based equity pairs trading with copula-based entry and exit signals on historical S&P 500 constituents over 1990–2015. It first fits ARMA–GARCH marginal return models, converts residuals to empirical uniform ranks, and estimates mixtures of Clayton, Frank or Student-t, and Gumbel copulas to capture asymmetric tail dependence. Conditional copula probabilities become daily mispricing indices whose cumulative deviations trigger trades. Twelve-month formation windows precede six-month trading windows. For the top five pairs on committed capital, reported after-cost annual excess returns are 3.68% for mixed copulas versus 2.30% for distance, with Sharpe ratios 0.58 versus 0.28. Results vary materially with portfolio breadth, capital denominator, subperiod, and trading frequency. The paper uses stationary bootstrap inference and factor regressions, but threshold sensitivity is examined over the full history, contemporaneous closes are assumed executable, and complex mixtures are selected by likelihood without a parameter penalty. These choices need stricter prospective controls in a new application.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Copula construction (pp. 7–10); cumulative indices explicitly need not be stationary (p. 9); signals/thresholds and committed versus invested capital (p. 11); data and execution assumptions (pp. 11–12); outcomes (pp. 14–16); trade-frequency comparison (pp. 18, 21); conclusions/remaining problems (pp. 27–28). For sports this is an optional dependence-model extension, not evidence that nonlinear dependence ensures reversion or tradability. A small sample of match contracts would not justify this parameter-heavy baseline. **Extraction:** Complete text and tables; some mathematical typos occur in the source/extraction, so conditional-probability indices should be independently derived before use.

### 5. Optimal Mean Reversion Trading with Transaction Costs and Stop-Loss Exit

**Authors/date:** Tim Leung and Xin Li, December 14, 2014. **Length:** 26 PDF pages.

Leung and Li formulate a sequential optimal-stopping problem: choose when to enter an OU spread and subsequently liquidate it, accounting for fixed entry and exit costs. Rather than guessing free boundaries, they transform rewards and construct their smallest concave majorants, which determine stopping and continuation regions. Without a stop-loss constraint, optimal entry and liquidation use lower and upper thresholds. Higher entry costs lower the entry threshold; higher exit costs raise the liquidation threshold. Imposing a lower stop-loss level changes the geometry: entry is optimal only within a bounded interval strictly above the stop, so a very low spread need not justify buying when forced liquidation is near. Tightening the stop also reduces the optimal take-profit level. An ETF example illustrates OU parameter estimation rather than proving profitable out-of-sample trading. The primary results are perpetual, continuous-diffusion, single-entry/single-exit calculations. Finite horizons, repeated trades, alternative dynamics, and relative stops require extensions; discrete jumps and missing liquidity invalidate a literal guaranteed stop-loss cap.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Estimation example and its limited objective (pp. 3–5); stopping objectives/cost assumptions (pp. 5–6); cost-dependent thresholds (pp. 9–13); stop-loss/take-profit interaction (pp. 14–16); bounded entry interval (pp. 17–19); finite-horizon extension explicitly left open (p. 20). For sports, this motivates evaluating cost-sensitive entry, exit, and no-trade regions under a finite pre-match deadline, with thresholds estimated only from training data. It does not authorize copying perpetual OU thresholds into expiring event contracts. **Extraction:** All text, derivations, and appendix readable; some equation layout is imperfect.

### 6. Optimal Multifactor Trading under Proportional Transaction Costs

**Author/date:** Richard J. Martin, May 1, 2012; arXiv v1 dated April 29, 2012. **Length:** 30 PDF pages.

Martin studies how a trader should adjust exposure when stochastic factors determine an asset's expected return and volatility and trading incurs proportional costs. The frictionless target balances expected profit against instantaneous variance. With costs, continuously chasing that target is prohibitively expensive, so the optimal policy has a no-trade region: keep the position unchanged inside a buffer and trade to its boundary outside it. A small-cost approximation makes buffer width proportional to the cube root of transaction cost and to the two-thirds power of the volatility of the target position, adjusted for the asset's own risk. Fast-changing signals therefore deserve wider buffers. Simulations and illustrative futures momentum examples support the approximation; a smaller displacement of the buffer center is derived but omitted in those examples. The objective is infinite-horizon utility of incremental P&L, not terminal wealth. Diffusive dynamics, ergodic factors, smoothness, proportional costs, and effectively unrestricted exposure limit transfer. Some strategies remain unattractive even after optimal buffering; optimizing execution cannot manufacture a missing net edge.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** No-trade logic (p. 2); objective and target (pp. 3–5); buffer rules and trading-speed sensitivity (pp. 6–9); high costs can invalidate the strategy (pp. 10–11); empirical implementation and discrete-time caveats (pp. 14–16); derivation (pp. 24–30). For sports, a no-trade band above spread, fees, and estimation error is relevant. The cube-root formula should not be transplanted to one-off bets or finite-lived contracts: the paper explicitly distinguishes single-period from continuous-trading cost behavior (p. 6). **Extraction:** All text/appendix present; mathematical layout is imperfect but core claims are stated clearly in prose.

### 7. Optimal Trading with Stochastic Liquidity and Volatility

**Author/date:** Robert Almgren, *SIAM Journal on Financial Mathematics* 3, 163–181, published January 31, 2012. DOI 10.1137/090763470. **Length:** 19 PDF pages. The filename reverses “liquidity” and “volatility.”

This paper concerns executing a predetermined quantity before a fixed deadline, balancing market-impact cost against the risk of waiting. The underlying price is a driftless arithmetic Brownian motion, while volatility and temporary linear price impact vary with an observable stochastic market state. A small-impact approximation discards uncertainty in impact costs relative to price risk, enabling dynamic programming. An HJB equation yields state-dependent optimal trading rates. The main numerical example assumes coordinated variation: volatility rises when market impact falls, so a trader accelerates during liquid, volatile periods and waits during illiquid, quiet periods. Deadline proximity eventually forces rapid completion. A rolling-horizon policy that treats current conditions as permanent is often an adequate approximation for urgent trades but responds less effectively for patient ones. This is an execution model rather than an alpha model, and it offers no evidence of profitable directional forecasts. The paper acknowledges that volatility and illiquidity can rise together during exceptional events, violating its simplifying assumption. Numerical illustrations, rather than a live-market strategy backtest, establish the behavior.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Mandatory target/deadline and distinction from short-term alpha (pp. 1–3); impact/slippage and variance criterion (pp. 3–5); coordinated variation and its failure in stressed markets (pp. 6–7); horizon uncertainty and small-impact approximation (p. 8); HJB (pp. 9–10); numerical execution trajectories (pp. 15–18). For sports, this supports explicitly pricing deadline and liquidity risk when planning a pre-match exit. It does not guarantee that a quoted exit exists or that a market order can be fully filled at a displayed price. **Extraction:** Complete text and figure captions; no missing pages. Formula spacing is sometimes imperfect.

### 8. Spread Trading Strategies in the Crude Oil Futures Market

**Author/date:** Thorben Manfred Lubnau, European University Viadrina Discussion Paper No. 353, April 2014. **Length:** 35 PDF pages (33 printed manuscript pages plus covers).

Lubnau tests Bollinger-band mean-reversion strategies on a WTI–Brent futures portfolio using daily settlement prices from 1992–2013. The spread hedge ratio is either fixed from a prior-year Johansen estimate or updated with a Gaussian dynamic linear model and Kalman filter; an additional version estimates state-noise variances by maximum likelihood. Signals use rolling standardized spread deviations, enter around two standard deviations, and exit at the moving mean or slightly beyond it. Performance is examined across overlapping five-year periods and compared with 1,000 randomized trading schedules matched on long/short trade counts and average holding periods. The paper reports strongest results for short lookbacks and the MLE/Kalman version, including unusually high win rates and Sharpe ratios. It also documents weakening cointegration and poorer recent results as the economics of WTI and Brent diverged. Results warrant replication: hedge updates must be represented as actual self-financing rebalancing, costs on those changes and futures rolls need explicit treatment, overlapping windows do not provide independent evidence, and random-entry tests do not fully address strategy-selection bias.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Cointegration disappears at conventional levels in the later subsample (p. 9, Table 3 p. 27); standardized signals and static hedge construction (pp. 9–11); causal filtering requirement and Kalman/MLE equations (pp. 12–14); randomized-entry benchmark (pp. 15–16); four-leg round-trip cost model (p. 17); structural-break discussion (p. 22); detailed outcomes (pp. 27–31). For identical sports payoffs, a fixed payoff-matching hedge is more defensible than estimating a changing hedge ratio; a Kalman filter could instead estimate a latent probability or discrepancy, with cash P&L computed from actual holdings. **Extraction:** Complete text, tables, references. All reported near-100% win rates are the paper's historical claims, not independently validated results.

### 9. Statistical Arbitrage in the U.S. Treasury Futures Market

**Author/date:** Wale Dare, University of St. Gallen Discussion Paper 2017-16, September 2017. **Length:** 38 PDF pages, including covers/front matter; final printed page 36.

Dare studies an intraday cointegration strategy involving 2-, 5-, 10-, and 30-year Treasury futures from April 2010 through December 2015. A factor-model discussion motivates common trends and stationary combinations; PCA on differenced log prices supplies supporting descriptive evidence. One month of one-minute quotes estimates a cointegrating portfolio and its moments, followed by roughly 100 trading hours. The strategy enters beyond two standard deviations, exits inside one standard deviation, and forcibly closes positions at the trading deadline. Signals use midquotes but simulated executions cross the bid–ask spread. Reported fully funded annualized excess returns are about 6%, whereas much larger returns on initial margin mainly reflect leverage. A parametric test on discounted gains supports the author's statistical-arbitrage claim. This is not proof of riskless profit or dominance: outcomes include losses, capital conventions materially affect performance, historical adjustment choices matter, and the inferential model assumes Gaussian innovations with restricted dependence. The paper itself acknowledges fat tails, misspecification, and the need for more robust inference.

**Useful methods and exact pages:** Actual quotes, sizes, and liquid-time restrictions (p. 8); futures stitching/artificial-jump risks (pp. 9–10); formation procedure (p. 23); midquote signals versus bid/ask execution and forced close (p. 25); capital-denominator problems (pp. 26–29); reported results and leverage interpretation (pp. 30–31); definition/test assumptions and explicit limitations (pp. 32–34). For sports, the strongest transferable elements are separating signal prices from executable prices, matching the observation/trading clock, and reporting returns on committed capital. Bounded finite-lived event probabilities are not automatically I(1) price series; standard cointegration claims need a different justification. **Extraction:** All pages contain usable text or figure captions; figure interiors on pp. 17, 19, 20 are not captured. They are descriptive PCA plots, and no exact plot value is used here. Several theoretical passages contain suspicious algebra or broad claims; they are not treated as independently verified theorems.

### Reading completion and methodological synthesis

All nine assigned papers were read through their final extracted-text pages, including references, appendices, tables, and available figure captions. No PDF in this group is missing or wholly unreadable. This is a full-text review, not a pixel-by-pixel reconstruction of every plot or a verification of every proof. Garbled display mathematics and figure-only information should be checked directly in the PDFs before numerical replication; the annotations above avoid relying on unreadable values.

The common methodological lessons are: establish an economically meaningful spread; distinguish statistical dependence from executable profit; reserve chronological formation/validation/testing periods; incorporate nonconvergence, finite deadlines, and structural breaks; separate latent signal estimation from cash P&L; and include spreads, fees, rebalancing, capital, and liquidity. A statistically significant average gain is not a sure arbitrage, and a negative after-cost result can still be a successful empirical project.

### 1. depmixS4: An R Package for Hidden Markov Models

**Ingmar Visser and Maarten Speekenbrink (2010).** *Journal of Statistical Software*, 36(7). **21 pages.** Local file: [An R Package for Hidden Markov Models.pdf](../papers/An%20R%20Package%20for%20Hidden%20Markov%20Models.pdf).

**Summary.** This software and methods paper presents a flexible framework for dependent mixture models: finite mixtures, latent classes, and hidden Markov models. A first-order latent state controls observation distributions, while covariates can affect response distributions, transition probabilities, and initial state probabilities. The authors derive the scaled forward likelihood recursion and expectation-maximization estimation, with direct numerical optimization available when linear parameter constraints make standard EM unsuitable. Worked examples distinguish fast, inaccurate responses from slower, more accurate responses in a psychological experiment; a separate latent-class example relates children's problem-solving strategies to age. Gaussian, multinomial, and other generalized-linear-model responses can be combined, and users can add distributions through the package's response interface. This is evidence about model specification and implementation, not trading profitability. Important limitations include sensitivity to starting values, local likelihood optimization, conditional independence when separate response models are listed, and the need to justify constraints. The paper explicitly distinguishes fitting a model from merely defining its specification.

**Relevant evidence and limits.** Model and likelihood: pp. 3–5; initialization/model selection: pp. 7–9; covariate-dependent transitions: pp. 9–10; conditional independence and constraints: pp. 11–13; extensibility: pp. 15–18. A latent liquidity or information-arrival regime is a possible later application, but its existence must be tested. For a tradable signal, use forward filtered state probabilities available at decision time; the full-sample forward-backward posterior used in EM includes later observations and cannot itself be a historical trading signal. The latter is a methodological implication of the conditioning sets on pp. 4–5.

### 2. Application of Support Vector Machine on Algorithmic Trading

**J. Szklarz, R. Rosillo, N. Alvarez, I. Fernández, and N. Garcia (2018).** *International Conference on Artificial Intelligence, ICAI'18*, printed pp. 400–406. **7 pages.** Local file: [Application of Support Vector Machine on Algorithmic Trading.pdf](../papers/Application%20of%20Support%20Vector%20Machine%20on%20Algorithmic%20Trading.pdf).

**Summary.** The paper compares support vector machines with conventional technical indicators and several other classifiers on S&P 500 trading. Inputs include contemporaneous and lagged RSI, MACD, VIX, and the index's daily change; labels depend on the index's direction five days later. Separate models are trained for upward and downward markets. The experiment uses two historical training periods and two approximately one-year testing periods, with a simulated $10,000 account and 0.35% commission per operation. Trading thresholds are optimized for Sharpe ratio, and optional gain/stop-loss and ADX filters modify orders. The reported SVM result is strongest during the declining test period, while buy-and-hold beats the trading algorithms during the rising period. However, the authors leave autonomous regime selection for future work, so the assumed availability of the correct trend-specific model needs scrutiny. Optimization and final testing are not separated clearly enough to establish a clean validation protocol. Several narrative claims about MACD losses and filter improvements conflict with the displayed positive returns or relative table rankings.

**Relevant evidence and limits.** Periods and inputs: pp. 3–4; labels, commissions, and all-capital positions: p. 4; threshold selection and filters: p. 5; results and inconsistencies: pp. 6–7. The transferable lesson is to compare a small number of methods under identical execution rules and costs. Neither the equity indicators nor hindsight labels for broad market regimes establish an edge in football markets. Outcome classification must be converted into an expected payoff at actual available odds; a high hit rate alone is insufficient.

### 3. Deep neural networks, gradient-boosted trees, random forests: Statistical arbitrage on the S&P 500

**Christopher Krauss, Xuan Anh Do, and Nicolas Huck (2016 working-paper version).** FAU Discussion Papers in Economics, No. 03/2016. **33 pages**, including two preliminary pages. Local file: [Deep neural networks gradient-boosted trees random forests.pdf](../papers/Deep%20neural%20networks%20gradient-boosted%20trees%20random%20forests.pdf).

**Summary.** This paper builds daily relative-value portfolios using neural networks, gradient-boosted trees, random forests, and their equal-weighted probability ensemble. A survivorship-adjusted S&P 500 dataset supplies 31 lagged-return features. Rolling 750-day training windows are followed by 250-day trading windows. The target is whether a stock outperforms the next day's cross-sectional median; each model ranks predicted probabilities, buying the top stocks and shorting the bottom stocks while excluding the uncertain middle. For ten stocks on each side, the ensemble reports average daily returns of approximately 0.45% before and 0.25% after its assumed transaction costs across December 1992–October 2015. The paper also measures tail risk, drawdowns, factor exposures, variable importance, and parameter sensitivity. Crucially, aggregate performance hides substantial deterioration: every model loses money after costs in January 2010–October 2015, even while gross returns remain positive. Dollar neutrality also fails to eliminate market exposure. The strongest lesson is that prediction, implementability, risk, and temporal stability require separate evaluation.

**Relevant evidence and limits.** Survivor treatment: p. 8; time splits, features, and labels: p. 9; ranking/abstention: pp. 14–15; costs and risks: pp. 15–19; performance deterioration: pp. 20–23, particularly Table 4 on p. 23; robustness: pp. 25–26. This provides a strong course bridge for systematic relative-value selection across event contracts. Its return magnitudes cannot be transferred to sports. ### 4. Deep Reinforcement Learning Pairs Trading

**Andrew Brim (2019).** Utah State University graduate creative project; internal title page dated May 2019, repository cover dated December 2019. **14 pages**, including repository cover. ### 5. Hidden Markov Model for Stock Selection

**Nguyet Nguyen and Dung Nguyen (2015).** *Risks*, 3, 455–473. DOI 10.3390/risks3040455. **19 pages.** Local file: [Hidden Markov Model for Stock Selection.pdf](../papers/Hidden%20Markov%20Model%20for%20Stock%20Selection.pdf).

**Summary.** This paper combines regime estimation with factor-based stock selection. Separate two-state Gaussian HMMs describe inflation, industrial production, the S&P 500, and VIX. Each month the algorithm calibrates the models, predicts the next regimes, and searches historical periods with comparable combinations of macro states. The past performance of five valuation/growth characteristics determines their weights in a composite stock score. The portfolio holds the highest-ranked 50 S&P 500 stocks and updates monthly. Over December 1999–December 2014, the authors report 14.9% annual growth versus 2.3% for the index, without transaction fees. The paper supplies introductory forward, backward, Viterbi, and Baum–Welch algorithms and detailed factor/portfolio plots. Its method offers an interpretable way to vary a strategy with market conditions. However, transaction costs are assumed negligible rather than measured, the main portfolio still suffers a reported 57.9% drawdown, and real-time availability of revised macro/fundamental data is not established. **Relevant evidence and limits.** Algorithms: pp. 3–6; data and factor design: pp. 7–9; rolling ranking procedure: p. 12; omitted costs and comparison: p. 15; portfolio risk: p. 16. ### 6. Tutorial: The Kalman Filter

**Tony Lacey (undated supplied chapter).** Chapter 11, printed pp. 133–140. **8 pages.** No publication year or enclosing book title is established by this file. Local file: [Kalman filter.pdf](../papers/Kalman%20filter.pdf).

**Summary.** This tutorial derives the Kalman filter as a recursive minimum-mean-square-error estimator. It begins with a noisy observation model and relates squared-error loss to Gaussian likelihood. The central state-space system separates an evolving hidden state from noisy observations, with distinct process and measurement covariance matrices. The new estimate equals the prior forecast plus a Kalman-gain-weighted innovation; minimizing the trace of the posterior error covariance yields the gain. State and covariance prediction then complete the recursion. A second derivation connects the update to weighted least squares, a chi-square merit function, and the inverse covariance or information matrix. This is a mathematical tutorial, without a trading dataset or empirical profitability result. Its usefulness is in causal estimation and explicit uncertainty accounting. The standard Gaussian linear formulation depends on correct dynamics, noise assumptions, and covariance calibration. Sudden news jumps, probability boundaries, or changing liquidity can violate those assumptions, and a smoothly estimated latent quantity is not automatically a tradable forecast.

**Relevant evidence and limits.** MSE/likelihood: pp. 1–2; state/measurement equations and independence assumptions: p. 3; gain and prediction recursion: pp. 4–6; information formulation: pp. 6–8. Possible later relevance is estimating a time-varying latent reference probability or residual level from noisy quotes. Process uncertainty and quote noise must be distinguished. Full-sample smoothing would leak future data into a historical signal; an online filter does not, provided its parameters were also fitted using available data. The extraction lost numerals, minus signs, and matrix symbols; all eight original pages were inspected visually before summarizing, and implementation should follow checked equations rather than the extracted text.

### 7. Tracking Problems, Hedge Fund Replication and Alternative Beta

**Thierry Roncalli and Guillaume Weisang (24 December 2008 version).** **66 pages**, including mathematical and algorithmic appendices. Stored under the misleading filename [particle-filter - Copy.pdf](../papers/particle-filter%20-%20Copy.pdf).

**Summary.** The authors formulate hedge-fund replication as a tracking problem: latent, evolving factor exposures generate observed fund returns. They first use linear Gaussian state-space models and Kalman filtering, then relax distributional and functional assumptions through particle filters, skew-t measurement noise, and option exposures with fixed or endogenous strikes. A six-factor liquid-asset tracker captures about 76% of reported annual hedge-fund-index performance over January 1994–September 2008, but results differ substantially across strategies. Increasing complexity often fails to improve tracking: matching higher moments raises tracking-error volatility, and nonlinear parameter estimation is expensive and unstable. The paper carefully distinguishes explanatory fit from feasible investment replication. It studies factor selection, correlation-driven identification problems, initial covariance and process-noise choices, and the effect of execution delay. Appendices derive Kalman and importance-sampling recursions and provide SIS, resampling, generic, SIR, and regularized particle-filter algorithms. The eventual core/satellite interpretation separates liquid replicable exposures from harder-to-replicate return components rather than claiming that all hedge-fund alpha has been eliminated.

**Relevant evidence and limits.** Tracking setup: pp. 7–12; innovations and tuning: pp. 13–17; detailed strategy comparisons: pp. 22–29; disappointing non-Gaussian results: pp. 31–32; sampling frequency and nonlinear replication: pp. 33–35; quoted versus traded pricing: p. 37; complexity/identification: pp. 39–40 and 50–60; latency experiment: pp. 40 and 43; particle degeneracy and algorithms: pp. 61–66. The particularly useful lesson for event contracts is that observed prices and estimated relationships are insufficient without timely executable instruments. A one-step implementation delay materially changes the tracker. Complex filters should only follow evidence that a simple filter is inadequate. Rotated tables on pp. 24–28 were read from rendered, rotated pages because their extracted text was scrambled.

### 8. Practical Deep Reinforcement Learning Approach for Stock Trading

### 9. Predicting the direction of stock market prices using random forest

**Luckyson Khaidem, Snehanshu Saha, and Sudeepa Roy Dey (2016 supplied manuscript, dated 29 April).** Header says “To appear in Applied Mathematical Finance,” but bibliographic placeholders remain; publication status is not established by this PDF. **20 pages**, despite the title header's placeholder “1–21.” Local file: [Predicting the Direction of Stock Market Price Using Random Forest.pdf](../papers/Predicting%20the%20Direction%20of%20Stock%20Market%20Price%20Using%20Random%20Forest.pdf).

**Summary.** The authors transform stock-direction forecasting into binary classification using random forests. Historical prices are exponentially smoothed, then technical indicators such as RSI, stochastic oscillator, Williams %R, MACD, rate of change, and on-balance volume form predictors. Targets indicate whether prices rise or fall after a chosen horizon. The paper explains tree splitting, bagging, ensemble voting, out-of-bag error, and a margin-based generalization bound; a worked example traces votes through individual trees. It reports roughly 85–95% accuracy for several stocks at monthly horizons and favorable comparisons with earlier SVM studies. These impressive classification figures do not constitute a realistic trading backtest: chronological train/test construction, separation of overlapping label horizons, and cost-adjusted portfolio performance are not adequately established. Out-of-bag errors can be optimistic for serially dependent data. The claim that overlapping convex hulls after two-dimensional PCA prove the original classification problem unsuitable for linear models is also too strong. Neither nonseparability nor the number of trees guarantees financially useful predictions.

**Relevant evidence and limits.** Smoothing and future-direction labels: pp. 3–5; PCA/separability argument: p. 6; tree/voting example and OOB: pp. 7–13; headline accuracy/ROC: pp. 14–19. A random forest is a feasible benchmark for nonlinear interactions between market and football features, but sports probabilities should be assessed for calibration and economic value at quoted prices. Match-level temporal splits prevent related observations from the same match entering both training and testing. The manuscript's strong accuracy claims should not become a target performance promise for a new project.

### Cross-paper course-method lessons

1. **Forecasting success is different from tradable success.** Krauss et al. show positive gross predictability alongside negative recent net performance; the random-forest and RL papers provide further reasons to separate accuracy/rewards from a cash-accounted strategy.
2. **Respect the information clock.** HMM smoothing, revised features, overlapping labels, regime selection, and ongoing model updates can introduce future information. Roncalli and Weisang directly quantify how implementation delay changes results.
3. **Start with a small, interpretable model.** Kalman filtering, a regime-conditioned threshold, or a small tree model can address a clear hypothesis. Particle filtering and deep RL add estimation and validation burdens and do not automatically improve results.
4. **Neutrality is a payoff property.** Dollar-neutral equity portfolios retain factor exposure. In event markets, matching stakes or correlating prices likewise does not prove hedging; contracts need outcome-by-outcome payoff accounting.
5. **No-trade decisions, costs, and uncertainty matter.** Excluding uncertain forecasts, penalizing adverse outcomes, measuring tail loss, and testing thresholds out of sample connect the ML readings directly to statistical-arbitrage practice.

### 1. A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing

**Authors/version:** Brian Hurst, Yao Hua Ooi, and Lasse H. Pedersen; AQR, Fall 2012. **Length:** 12 PDF pages, including a blank second page. **File:** `Papers/A_Century_of_Evidence_on_Trend-Following_Investing.pdf`.

**Summary.** The authors reconstruct a simple time-series momentum strategy from January 1903 to June 2012 across 59 markets: 24 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bond markets, and nine currencies. They average signals based on one-, three-, and twelve-month past returns, allocate approximately equal volatility to individual markets, and scale the combined portfolio to a 10% annual volatility target. Historical futures returns are supplemented with financed cash-market proxies where necessary. Simulations include estimated transaction costs and hypothetical management/performance fees. The full-sample net-of-fee Sharpe ratio is 1.00, and every decade has positive reported returns. The strategy also earns positive returns in nine of the ten worst historical drawdowns of a 60/40 portfolio. These are hypothetical results, with substantial dependence on historical proxies, cost estimates, diversification, and volatility scaling. The transferable lessons are to specify a simple benchmark, compare market regimes, and measure net rather than gross returns. The study does not establish that short-lived, bounded event-contract prices trend, nor justify transplanting its monthly horizons into sports trading.

- **Methods/results:** PDF p. 3 gives signals, universe, covariance estimation, and position sizing; p. 4 gives the decade results; pp. 5–7 analyze drawdowns, diversification, and strategy capacity.
- **Reproducibility/limits:** pp. 8–10 describe instruments, historical data sources, and modeled transaction costs; p. 12 explicitly distinguishes hypothetical performance from achievable results. A long history does not itself eliminate all selection or implementation biases.

### 2. A Primer on Alternative Risk Premia

**Authors/version:** Rayann Hamdan, Fabien Pavlowsky, Thierry Roncalli, and Ban Zheng; April 2016. **Length:** 123 PDF pages. **File:** `Papers/Alternative_Risk_Premia.pdf`.

**Summary.** This extensive survey separates risk premia, which compensate investors for adverse-state exposure, from return-explaining risk factors and apparent market anomalies. It reviews carry, liquidity, momentum, reversal, value, volatility, and equity-specific strategies. Starting from approximately 2,000 commercial products, the authors use qualitative screening and a similarity-based deletion algorithm to construct 59 generic strategy indices. They compare returns, skewness, drawdowns, correlations, and nonlinear dependence on equity and bond benchmarks. A central result is that diversification of ordinary volatility can coexist with severe common losses: adding negatively skewed strategies need not diversify their jump or tail exposure. The paper also applies lasso selection to hedge-fund replication, including a rolling out-of-sample exercise. Its indices and classification choices remain implementation- and sample-dependent, and some unusually strong backtests prompt the authors' own skepticism. For sports-event portfolios, the useful extension is to examine outcome concentration, joint adverse scenarios, and tail losses alongside any variance constraint. Positive average betting returns would require evidence of an exploitable mechanism; they are not automatically a risk premium or an arbitrage.

- **Concepts:** pp. 3–19 distinguish CAPM/APT, stochastic discount factors, bad times, skewness, and anomaly claims; pp. 21–33 define strategy families.
- **Empirical construction:** pp. 33–43 describe filtering and the 59 indices; pp. 75–85 give the database and selection algorithm. The quantitative procedure removes indices least similar to peers, with a minimum population and an R-squared threshold; this is a definition of a representative index, not proof of tradable alpha.
- **Core project connection:** pp. 45–55 compare ordinary diversification with skewness aggregation, nonlinear payoff fitting, and scenario analysis. The particularly negative skew and drawdown ratio of US equity volatility carry appear in Table 5, p. 42.
- **Estimation and appendices:** pp. 55–65 present lasso and rolling 24-month exposures; pp. 86–105 derive reversal/OU and trend-following P&L, time aggregation, and coskewness; pp. 106–123 provide additional results. Several convexity labels and displayed formulas in this version appear inconsistent with their accompanying equations/plots, so an implementation should derive its own formulas rather than copy them mechanically.

### 3. Arbitrage-Free Parametric Volatility Surfaces and Real-Time Fitting

**Author/version:** Timothy R. Klassen, Vola Dynamics; Global Derivatives Chicago, November 2, 2017. **Length:** 63 slides. **File:** `Papers/Arbitrage-Free Parametric Volatility Surface and real time fitting.pdf`.

**Summary.** This practitioner presentation explains how to fit option-implied volatility curves and surfaces while maintaining price consistency. It begins with dividend modeling and American-to-European option conversion, then separates the overall volatility level from dimensionless curve-shape parameters. Butterfly-arbitrage restrictions require nonnegative implied density; calendar restrictions constrain total variance across maturity. The slides discuss three-parameter S3/SSVI curves, more flexible alternatives, and why very liquid or event-sensitive markets may require richer shapes. Real-time fitting combines uncertainty-weighted errors, soft penalties, cross-maturity information, and temporal filtering. Examples show that a curve can fit quoted points plausibly while still generating economically inconsistent prices, particularly with sparse or illiquid quotes. Some fitting families are proprietary and the presentation is not a fully reproducible empirical study. Its strongest sports-market connection is the discipline of enforcing consistent payoffs and accounting for quote uncertainty before labeling discrepancies as arbitrage. Option-surface equations do not directly apply to three-way football match outcomes. In particular, a probability or midpoint inconsistency is insufficient evidence of an executable profit after bid–ask spreads and settlement differences.

- **Theory:** slides 4–7 cover dividends; 9–15 cover parameterization and no-arbitrage restrictions; 17–24 discuss S3/SSVI, wings, and additional flexibility.
- **Implementation:** slides 25–26 describe weighted fitting; 27–40 present empirical fits and temporal filtering; 41–61 demonstrate arbitrage elimination and illiquid-name fitting. Slide 54 specifically warns about apparent arbitrage in midprices when spreads are ignored.
- **Limit:** the deck's claims concern its option-fitting framework; slides 62–63 identify omitted issues and summarize commercial implementation claims rather than establish a sports-market result.

### 4. Realized Volatility

**Authors/version:** Torben G. Andersen and Luca Benzoni; July 22, 2008 draft, Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago WP 2008-14, prepared for the *Handbook of Financial Time Series*. **Length:** 29 PDF pages; substantive chapter is pp. 2–16, references pp. 16–22, followed by a working-paper catalog. **File:** `Papers/Realized Volatility.pdf`.

**Summary.** The chapter develops realized volatility as an ex-post measure of return variation constructed from sums of high-frequency squared returns. Under frictionless semimartingale assumptions, increasing observation frequency makes realized variation converge to quadratic variation. With continuous paths this equals integrated variance; with jumps it also includes squared jump sizes. Crucially, denser observations over a fixed calendar span do not similarly identify expected returns: estimating drift still requires a long time span. The authors distinguish realized variation from an ex-ante conditional variance forecast and explain how realized bipower variation separates continuous variation from finite-activity jumps. Practical sections cover discretization, bid–ask bounce, stale/non-synchronous prices, volatility signature plots, subsampling, and kernel approaches. The chapter is a methodological survey, not a strategy profitability claim. For event contracts, it supports measuring quote noise and announcement-related jumps, and choosing sampling intervals empirically. Thin trading and settlement at zero or one complicate direct use of equity-style log returns. Moreover, variability of quoted probabilities is different from the terminal Bernoulli payoff risk of a held bet.

- **Main distinction:** PDF pp. 4–5 derive the drift-versus-volatility estimation contrast; pp. 6–9 define quadratic variation, realized variation, and conditional variance.
- **Jumps and noise:** pp. 9–10 cover jump and bipower decomposition; pp. 11–12 discuss microstructure distortions and remedies; pp. 14–15 discuss multivariate asynchronous trading.
- **Forecasting:** pp. 13–14 describe persistence and a daily/weekly/monthly realized-variation regression. These calendar scales are examples from financial markets, not defaults for sports data.

### 5. Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism: The Effects of Backtest Overfitting on Out-of-Sample Performance

**Authors/version:** David H. Bailey, Jonathan M. Borwein, Marcos López de Prado, and Qiji Jim Zhu; *Notices of the AMS*, May 2014, 61(5), pp. 458–471. **Length:** 14 PDF pages. **File:** `Papers/The Effects of Backtest Overfitting on Out-of_Sample Performance.pdf`.

**Summary.** The authors show how searching many strategy configurations manufactures impressive in-sample performance even when all candidates are driven by uninformative random data. Using the distribution of the maximum of multiple estimated Sharpe ratios, they derive an approximate minimum backtest length that increases with the number of independent trials. They emphasize that overfitting occurs throughout a strategy, including entry thresholds, stops, holding periods, and sizing, rather than only inside its forecasting model. Simulations distinguish memoryless returns, where selection fails to improve expected out-of-sample performance, from constrained or mean-reverting cumulative performance, where selection can actively worsen it. A practical example searches 8,800 seasonal trading configurations on a random walk and finds an apparently persuasive Sharpe ratio of 1.27. The minimum-length calculation relies on simplifying assumptions and is a diagnostic, not a guarantee. For a sports project, every league subset, entry time, odds band, FPL feature, and threshold tried belongs in the research-trial record. A small predetermined hypothesis set and untouched chronological evaluation are much more credible than optimizing until profits appear.

- **Key references:** PDF pp. 4–6 develop expected maximum Sharpe and minimum backtest length; pp. 7–9 provide simulation mechanisms; pp. 10–11 give the seasonal-rule example and conclusions; pp. 12–13 provide proofs.
- **Interpretation limit:** the annualized numerical examples assume particular sampling and distributional conditions. They should not be converted mechanically into a required number of football seasons or independent matches.

### 6. The Probability of Backtest Overfitting

**Authors/version:** David H. Bailey, Jonathan M. Borwein, Marcos López de Prado, and Qiji Jim Zhu; supplied working-paper draft dated February 12, 2014, first version August 2013. **Length:** 42 PDF pages. **File:** `Papers/The probability of Backtest Overfitting.pdf`.

**Summary.** The paper defines the probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) as the probability that the strategy selected as best in-sample ranks below the median candidate out-of-sample. Its combinatorially symmetric cross-validation procedure first forms a synchronized return matrix for all tested strategies, divides its rows into an even number of blocks, and repeatedly selects on one half of the blocks while evaluating on the complement. The out-of-sample relative rank is transformed to log-odds; the mass below zero estimates PBO. Complementary diagnostics measure performance degradation, loss probability, and stochastic dominance. Simulated null and genuine seasonal examples illustrate why similarly high in-sample Sharpe ratios can have very different selection reliability. PBO is not the probability that a strategy loses money and is not proof of future profitability. The authors explicitly say it cannot correct look-ahead, wrong costs, omitted trials, or unseen structural breaks. Applied to sports, blockwise diagnostics could supplement a chronological holdout if the sample and candidate set are adequate. They should not replace forward evaluation or be optimized as another selection objective.

- **Procedure:** PDF pp. 5–10 define the ranking-based estimand and algorithm; pp. 10–17 distinguish loss probability, degradation, and dominance.
- **Limits essential to a project:** pp. 18–21 discuss block size, candidate granularity, full trial reporting, incorrect backtests, regime change, and misuse of PBO as an optimization target.
- **Evidence:** pp. 21–29 compare controlled simulations and extreme-value calculations; pp. 30–38 contrast artificial seasonal examples (reported PBO about 55% versus 13%); pp. 39–40 conclude. These are examples, not universal cutoffs.

### 7. Trading Strategies with L1 Filtering

**Authors/version:** Benjamin Bruder, Tung-Lam Dao, and Thierry Roncalli; March 2011. **Length:** 22 PDF pages. **File:** `Papers/Trading-L1-Filtering.pdf`.

**Summary.** This paper estimates a latent signal by minimizing squared fitting error plus an L1 penalty on discrete derivatives. Penalizing second differences produces a piecewise-linear trend; penalizing first differences produces piecewise-constant levels. Combining the penalties allows local trends and changing mean levels. The authors provide simulation examples, a quadratic-program dual, computational advice, and rolling training/validation procedures for choosing regularization at an intended forecasting horizon. They then combine local and global trends in a capped long/short S&P 500 allocation whose exposure depends on estimated drift divided by variance. In the 1998–2010 exercise, the global L1 signal reports a 6.95% annual performance and 0.19 Sharpe ratio, against 2.04% and −0.06 for the index; competing filters give similar results. This is modest, sample-specific evidence with no clear transaction-cost analysis, rather than a universal advantage. For sports markets, first-difference penalties could distinguish changing quote levels from noise and second-difference penalties could supply a momentum baseline. Every historical signal must be refitted using only information available at the trade time; a whole-sample smooth line is not tradable evidence.

- **Methods:** PDF pp. 2–7 define the penalties and synthetic tests; pp. 9–12 explain regularization scaling, rolling validation, and local/global switching.
- **Backtest:** pp. 13–15 specify and report the S&P strategy; pp. 17–22 contain dual optimization and computational details.
- **Cautions:** p. 12 reports much weaker short-horizon direction prediction than longer-horizon prediction. The supplied text contains notation and horizon inconsistencies (for example, calling 520 trading days one year); reproduce the intended method explicitly instead of copying such labels.

### 8. Trend Detection and Data Mining via Wavelet and Hilbert-Huang Transforms

**Authors/version:** Murat Yasar and Asok Ray; 2008 American Control Conference, June 11–13, 2008, pp. 4292–4297. **Length:** seven PDF pages, including a ResearchGate cover sheet. **File:** `Papers/Trend Detection and Data Mining  via Wavelet and Hilbert-Huang Transforms.pdf`.

**Summary.** This engineering paper compares two methods for detecting slowly developing faults in noisy, nonlinear, nonstationary systems. Wavelet-transform symbolic encoding first maps observations into time-frequency coefficients, partitions them into a finite symbol alphabet, and monitors state-probability vectors. Hilbert-Huang analysis instead uses empirical mode decomposition followed by Hilbert transforms to examine instantaneous frequency and energy. Validation uses a simulated forced Duffing oscillator whose dissipation parameter increases gradually from 0.10 to 0.35 over 400 seconds, with added Gaussian noise. Both methods identify changing behavior even when phase plots overlap and the dominant frequencies remain similar; the Hilbert-Huang representation reveals declining low-frequency energy. The implementation timings demonstrate computational feasibility on the tested hardware, not trading feasibility or profitability. The work offers possible tools for detecting changing market regimes, but contains no financial prices, betting observations, return forecasts, or execution tests. For a small sports project, these methods would be optional diagnostics at most. Their many design choices and boundary effects require care, and any transformation must be constructed causally before it can enter a backtest.

- **Methods:** PDF pp. 3–4 introduce wavelets, Hilbert transforms, empirical mode decomposition, and symbolic encoding; p. 5 states both algorithms.
- **Validation/limits:** pp. 6–7 give the Duffing experiment, eight-state encoding, ten-second rolling window, 0.5-second updates, computation times, and future work on non-Gaussian disturbances. The abstract's broad noise ambitions should not be mistaken for validation across all such noise types.

### 9. Trend Filtering Methods for Momentum Strategies

**Authors/version:** Benjamin Bruder, Tung-Lam Dao, Jean-Charles Richard, and Thierry Roncalli; December 2011. **Length:** 49 PDF pages. The actual title ends in **Strategies**, although the supplied filename ends in **Trading**. **File:** `Papers/Trend Filtering Methods for Momentum Trading.pdf`.

**Summary.** This survey compares moving averages, crossover filters, least-squares/Hodrick–Prescott filters, Kalman state-space models, local regression, L1 penalties, wavelets, and multivariate common-trend methods. Appendices develop Kalman smoothing, optimization, wavelets, support-vector regression, and singular-spectrum analysis. Its central distinction is between estimating a visually smooth historical trend and demonstrating that the trend predicts future returns. The authors explicitly illustrate that a driftless random walk can have convincing ex-post trends. They discuss causal filters, estimator variance, trend-detection tests, and calibration matched to the desired forecasting horizon. S&P 500 illustrations compare filters, and a limited multi-market exercise compares subsequent monthly returns after positive versus negative three-month moves. These examples do not establish a complete net-profit trading system. The sports connection is strong at the research-design level: use a simple causal baseline, estimate uncertainty, and test whether a discrepancy predicts a subsequent price move or settlement outcome. Jointly observed venue prices may be modeled with a common latent component, but finite-lived event contracts do not automatically satisfy financial-price integration and cointegration assumptions.

- **Causality and estimation:** PDF pp. 4–13 explain causal linear filters, drift estimation, and Kalman filtering versus smoothing; pp. 14–21 survey nonlinear and multivariate approaches.
- **Validation:** pp. 22–25 connect calibration to forecast horizon; pp. 25–29 discuss estimator uncertainty and trend detection; pp. 30–31 explicitly distinguish apparent trends from forecastability.
- **Portfolio limitations:** p. 32 says sizing, correlation, risk budgets, execution, and transaction costs remain additional design problems. Statistical appendices occupy pp. 33–44; references pp. 45–49.

### Lessons to carry into the later proposal

1. **Specify what is predicted and what is traded.** A smoothed probability path, a predicted settlement probability, and an executable price discrepancy are different objects.
2. **Use information available at decision time.** Rolling filters can be valid; retrospective full-sample smoothing, revised features, and asynchronous quote comparisons can introduce future information.
3. **Retain realistic execution.** Use executable sides, commissions, spreads, depth, timing, and matched settlement conditions. Midpoint disagreement alone is not arbitrage.
4. **Control the research search.** Keep all tried configurations, a limited hypothesis set, and chronological testing. PBO is an optional selection diagnostic with sample requirements, not a substitute for sound data or an untouched test period.
5. **Measure event-level risk.** More ticks do not create more independent match outcomes. Variance and Sharpe ratios should be supplemented with drawdown, tail-loss, concentration, and adverse-outcome scenarios.
6. **Treat transferability as a hypothesis.** The financial trend/reversal papers motivate methods; the engineering paper motivates signal analysis. None establishes that football prices follow an OU process or that FPL information creates profitable betting signals.

### AGame-TheoreticalApproachforDesigningMarketTrading STrategies.pdf

**Actual title:** *A Game-Theoretical Approach for Designing Market Trading Strategies*. Garrison W. Greenwood and Richard Tymerski, IEEE CIG, 2008. **7 pages.**

The authors coevolve two populations of fuzzy trading rules rather than optimize a single rule population. Inputs describe narrow-range days, doji patterns, and hook days; fuzzy membership functions allow partial matches. A rule matrix produces a trading desirability score, and the example buys at the open and sells at the close when desirability exceeds 0.8. The evolutionary tournament transfers a fraction of simulated wealth between competing strategies. Of 1,600 available trading days, the first 1,000 are assigned to training and the last 600 to testing. In two illustrated test windows the fuzzy strategy earns less than the simple strategy in the rising market but preserves capital in the falling market. Training account balances partly reflect artificial tournament transfers, not just trading returns, and the selected illustrations are not a comprehensive profitability study.

**Pages and relevance:** pp. 3–5 explain features and rules; pp. 5–7 explain training and results. Useful for distinguishing model-selection objectives from actual trading profit and for benchmarking active trading against a simple policy. Coevolution would introduce substantial search flexibility for a small football sample; this paper does not establish a sports-market edge.

### Dealing with the Inventory Risk Market Making.pdf

**Actual title:** *Dealing with the Inventory Risk: A solution to the market making problem*. Olivier Guéant, Charles-Albert Lehalle, Joaquin Fernandez-Tapia. Supplied draft July 2012, arXiv version August 2012. **36 pages.**

This paper solves a market maker's finite-horizon exponential-utility problem with hard inventory limits. The reference price follows arithmetic Brownian motion and market orders arrive with intensities decreasing exponentially in the distance of the dealer's quotes from that reference. A change of variables converts the coupled HJB equations into a finite linear ODE system. A spectral characterization yields asymptotic quotes and approximations; extensions allow price drift and adverse-selection-like impact. Quotes respond jointly to inventory, volatility, arrival intensity, and risk aversion. The historical illustration uses France Telecom on one day and compares the strategy with a dealer quoting at the best bid and ask. Its execution simulation assumes full fills when trade prices reach the dealer's quote; the authors explicitly withhold full algorithmic details.

**Pages and relevance:** model pp. 4–7; approximations/extensions pp. 10–20; backtest assumptions pp. 20–23; proofs pp. 25–36. Supports explicit inventory and execution constraints. Applying its formulas to bounded, expiring match contracts would require a different price/terminal-payoff model. Quotes being touched is insufficient evidence of a real fill when queue position is unknown.

### Frequency Arbitrage.pdf

**Actual item:** *Frequency Arbitrage*. No author or date printed. **1-page teaching handout, not a complete research article.**

The handout plots S&P 500 historical volatility against sampling interval over two estimation periods and observes lower measured volatility at longer intervals. It motivates combining otherwise offsetting option positions whose delta hedges are rebalanced at different frequencies. After canceling the option positions, the remaining index exposure produces an intraperiod mean-reversion strategy. An exercise asks the reader to derive exposure proportional to the difference between reciprocal current price and reciprocal price at the start of the week. It does not supply a proof, calibrated implementation, cost analysis, or out-of-sample evaluation. The page was also inspected visually to read the graphs and formula.

**Relevance:** motivates measuring signals at multiple sampling frequencies and examining rebalancing costs. Different sampling intervals can also reflect microstructure effects. The handout cannot justify assuming football probabilities must revert or treating every volatility-frequency difference as a tradable arbitrage.

### MorrisonPike77.pdf

**Actual title:** *Kalman Filtering Applied to Statistical Forecasting*. G. W. Morrison and D. H. Pike, *Management Science* 23(7), March 1977, pp. 768–774. **7 scanned pages.**

The paper expresses a noisy time series with a time-varying mean as a linear state-space system. The latent state contains the mean and coefficients of known functions; it illustrates constant coefficients, random parameter movement, serially correlated changes, and a growth-rate model. Kalman recursion updates estimates and their uncertainty from each new observation. Ordinary least squares emerges as a special case, while fixed-dimensional recursion and moving coefficients give the filter computational and modeling advantages. An electricity-demand example initializes on fourteen annual observations and compares subsequent one-step forecasts. The reported sum of squared errors is 22.225 for the stationary linear specification, 1.286 for the stationary quadratic specification, and 0.335 for a nonstationary specification. The improvement comes from the chosen model, not a universal superiority of filtering.

**Pages and relevance:** assumptions pp. 1–3; recursion/optimality qualifications pp. 3–5; example pp. 5–7. Provides a clear basis for sequential estimation of changing latent probabilities and uncertainty. Gaussian/linear assumptions require checking after transforming bounded event probabilities; observation noise and process noise are distinct.

### Optimal FX Market Making under Inventory Risk.pdf

**Actual title:** *Optimal FX Market Making under Inventory Risk and Adverse Selection Constraints*. Sid Ghoshal and Stephen Roberts, September 2015. **12 pages.**

The authors combine an inventory-based quoting framework with a proposed adjustment for adverse selection by particular FX counterparties. They fit automatic-relevance-determination Gaussian processes to client trading behavior, compare the resulting distributions using Bhattacharyya distances, and group brokers using Bayesian nonnegative matrix factorization. Their main empirical subset contains 35,001 EUR/USD trades during January 2013; the clustering application uses 25 broker connections. A Gaussian entropy measure is proposed as a way to widen quotes for less predictable clients. The crucial premise that high entropy identifies adverse selectors is an assumption, and the functional form of the quoting adjustment is left as an open question. The paper provides behavior analysis and a proposed integration, not a completed out-of-sample trading-profit demonstration.

**Pages and relevance:** framework pp. 2–3; data/features pp. 3–5; empirical analysis pp. 6–10; proposed adjustment p. 11. Future-return and forward-volatility features are retrospective diagnostics, not contemporaneously available inputs. For betting, news shocks and stale quotes create analogous adverse-selection concerns; counterparty-specific FX models cannot be reproduced without equivalent private data.

### TimeSeriesPredictionbyKalmanSmoother.pdf

**Actual title:** *Time Series Prediction by Kalman Smoother with Cross-Validated Noise Density*. Simo Särkkä, Aki Vehtari, Jouko Lampinen, IJCNN CATS benchmark, 2004. **5 pages.**

The method combines a locally linear long-run signal with an autoregressive model for short-run residual periodicity. Kalman filtering and backward smoothing estimate the long-run signal, time-varying AR weights, and short-run component in successive stages. Cross-validation selects process-noise parameters to target the benchmark's prediction criterion. The figures illustrate reconstruction of missing intervals and prediction at the end of the series. As an interval moves away from observed data, the short-run oscillatory contribution weakens toward the long-run estimate. This is a time-series reconstruction application, not a trading backtest, and the paper does not demonstrate investment profitability.

**Pages and relevance:** p. 2 explicitly distinguishes filtering conditional on observations through the current time from smoothing conditional on the entire sample; model and tuning pp. 2–3; results pp. 3–5. Useful for separating slow latent movement from noisy prices and tuning uncertainty parameters. A tradable signal must use one-sided filtering, or smoothing restricted to data already available at the decision time. Full-sample smoothed historical signals would leak future information.
